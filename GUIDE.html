The project in one paragraph

We study 137 leukaemia patients who had a bone marrow transplant. After the transplant, one of three things is recorded first: the leukaemia comes back (relapse), the patient dies before any relapse (death before relapse), or the study ends with neither (censored). Our question is: which patients are more likely to relapse, and how do we measure that risk correctly when some patients die first? We describe the data, estimate survival, show that the simple method overestimates relapse, fit two regression models, and confirm the point with a simulation.

Basic ideas

Survival (time-to-event) data

For each patient we record how long until an event happens, not only whether it happened. Example: "relapsed on day 248". Columns come in pairs: a time (days) and an indicator (1 = event happened, 0 = not).

Censoring

Sometimes follow-up ends before the event happens: the study closes, or the patient moves away. We know the patient was event-free up to their last day, but nothing after that. That patient is censored.

We cannot delete censored patients (we would lose information) and we cannot count their last day as an event (that would be false). Survival methods use them correctly: they count as "at risk" until their last day and then leave the calculation.

Competing risks

A competing event is one that makes the event of interest impossible. Here, a patient who dies before relapsing can never relapse. Death "competes" with relapse.

The difference from censoring is important:

Censored patient Patient who died first
Could they still relapse later? Yes, we just stopped watching No, never
How methods should treat them Remove from the risk set, assume they could still relapse Count as a separate event

An easy way to explain it: imagine three alarm clocks beside each patient - relapse, death, end of study. Whichever rings first is the only one we hear.

How this appears in the data

01_preprocessing.R creates one outcome column, event_status:

event_status Meaning
1 Relapse (event of interest)
2 Death before relapse (competing event)
0 Censored

The time for all three is time (the original t2 column: days to relapse, death or end of follow-up).

Hazard

The hazard is the instant rate of the event: among patients still event-free today, how likely is the event to happen right now? Regression models for survival data describe how covariates change the hazard.

File by file

01_preprocessing.R - preparing the data

  • Installs any missing packages and loads them.
  • Loads bmt from the KMsurv package.
  • Renames cryptic columns (z1 becomes patient_age, t2 becomes dfs_days, and so on). The full list is in the comments at the top of the file.
  • Turns 0/1 codes into labels (0/1 becomes Female/Male). R then treats them as categories (factors).
  • Creates event_status, relapse_event and death_event.
  • Creates age10 = age / 10, so model results read "per 10 years older" instead of "per 1 year", which is easier to interpret.
  • Checks that our outcome agrees with the dataset's own d3 column (should print TRUE).

02_eda.R - exploratory data analysis

EDA means looking at the data before modelling it, to understand who the patients are and spot anything unusual. - Baseline summary table - one row per disease group: number of patients, median ages, waiting time, and the percentage who are male, CMV positive, received MTX, relapsed, or died first. - Bar charts - how many patients are in each disease group and each category. - Boxplots - the box shows the middle 50% of values, the line inside is the median, and the whiskers show the spread. Used for age and waiting time. - First event by group - the share of patients in each group who relapsed, died first, or were censored. - Event timing - when relapses and deaths happened after transplant.

03_kaplan_meier.R - Kaplan-Meier survival curves

Question: what percentage of patients are still alive at each day after transplant?

How it works: the curve starts at 100%. Each time a patient dies, it steps down by the share of patients still at risk who died that day. Censored patients leave the "at risk" group without making the curve drop; they are shown as small + marks.

Why it is fine here: for overall survival, death is the only event, so there is no competing risk.

Log-rank test: checks whether the survival curves of the three disease groups differ more than chance would explain. The null hypothesis is "all groups have the same survival". A p-value below 0.05 is evidence that they differ.

Risk table: the numbers under the plot are how many patients were still being followed at each time.

Key code:

km_fit <- survfit(Surv(os_days, died) ~ disease_group, data = analysis_data)
survdiff(Surv(os_days, died) ~ disease_group, data = analysis_data)

Surv(time, event) builds the survival outcome. survfit() estimates the curves; survdiff() runs the log-rank test.

04_cumulative_incidence.R - relapse vs death

Question: by a given day, what percentage of patients have relapsed, taking into account that some died first?

The cumulative incidence function (CIF) gives exactly that probability. It is estimated with the Aalen-Johansen method. At each event time it adds: (probability of still being event-free just before) x (relapses at that time / patients at risk). Because deaths reduce the "still event-free" part, dead patients correctly stop contributing to relapse.

Why not 1 - Kaplan-Meier for relapse? If we run Kaplan-Meier on relapse and treat deaths as censored, the method assumes those patients could still relapse later. The estimate of relapse comes out too high. The plot cif_naive_vs_aalen_johansen.png shows the gap, and the script prints both numbers at one year.

Gray's test: the competing-risks version of the log-rank test. It checks whether the cumulative incidence differs between disease groups. Row 1 of the output is relapse, row 2 is death before relapse.

Key code:

cuminc(ftime = analysis_data$time, fstatus = analysis_data$event_status,
       group = analysis_data$disease_group, cencode = 0)
timepoints(cif_overall, times = c(100, 365, 730))

cuminc() (package cmprsk) estimates the curves and runs Gray's test. timepoints() reads the estimates at chosen days.

05_cox_model.R - Model 1: cause-specific Cox model

Question: among patients who are still alive and relapse-free, which factors make relapse happen faster?

How it works: the Cox proportional hazards model relates the hazard of relapse to the covariates (disease group, age per 10 years, sex). Deaths are treated as censored, which is correct for this question because we only look at patients still able to relapse.

Output - hazard ratio (HR): - HR = 1: no effect - HR = 2: relapse rate twice as high as the reference group - HR = 0.5: relapse rate half as high

Assumption - proportional hazards: each factor's effect is assumed to stay the same over time. cox.zph() tests this. A p-value above 0.05 means no evidence of a problem. The "GLOBAL" row tests the whole model at once.

Key code:

coxph(Surv(time, relapse_event) ~ disease_group + age10 + patient_sex, data = analysis_data)
cox.zph(cox_relapse)

06_fine_gray_model.R - Model 2: Fine-Gray model

Question: which factors change the overall probability of relapse (the cumulative incidence), counting that some patients die first?

How it works: patients who die stay in the calculation as "not relapsed", so the model describes the real-world chance of relapse.

Output - subdistribution hazard ratio (SHR): read it like an HR. Above 1 means a higher probability of relapse, below 1 a lower one.

Key code:

fg_covariates <- model.matrix(~ disease_group + age10 + patient_sex, data = analysis_data)[, -1]
crr(ftime = analysis_data$time, fstatus = analysis_data$event_status,
    cov1 = fg_covariates, failcode = 1, cencode = 0)

crr() needs the covariates as numbers, so model.matrix() turns categories into 0/1 columns (for example disease_groupAML low risk = 1 for AML low-risk patients). failcode = 1 says relapse is the event of interest.

07_model_comparison.R - comparing the two models

Puts HR and SHR side by side and draws a forest plot.

Why use both models? They answer different questions:

Cause-specific Cox Fine-Gray
Question How fast does relapse happen among those still at risk? What is the overall chance of relapse?
Effect measure Hazard ratio (HR) Subdistribution hazard ratio (SHR)
Patients who died Removed from the risk set Kept in as "not relapsed"
Best for Understanding causes Prediction and patient counselling

The two can differ. For example, a factor that raises the death rate removes patients before they can relapse. Fine-Gray may then show a lower overall relapse probability even if Cox shows no effect on the relapse rate. Reporting both gives the full picture, as recommended by Putter et al. (2007).

08_simulation.R - simulation study

Why simulate? In real data we never know the true relapse risk, so we cannot check which method is right. In a simulation we create data where the true answer is known.

Design: - Each simulated dataset has 137 patients (same as the real data). - Each patient gets a random time to relapse, to death and to censoring, from exponential distributions. The earliest one is what we "observe". - Because the rates are known, the true probability of relapse by time 5 can be calculated exactly with a formula. - Six scenarios: three death rates (low, medium, high competition) x two censoring levels. 500 datasets per scenario. - In each dataset both methods estimate the relapse probability at time 5.

Performance measures: - Bias = average estimate minus the true value. 0 is ideal. - RMSE (root mean squared error) = typical size of the error, combining bias and variability. Smaller is better.

Expected result: Aalen-Johansen has bias close to 0 in every scenario. 1 - Kaplan-Meier is too high, and the gap grows as the death rate increases. With very few deaths, both methods agree.

set.seed() fixes the random numbers so that everyone who runs the script gets the same results.

The Shiny app

app.R is an interactive version of the analysis. You do not need to run the scripts first.

How Shiny works: - ui describes what the page looks like: tabs, menus, sliders and empty areas for plots and tables. - server contains the R code that fills each area. Each output is written as output$name <- renderPlot({ ... }) and reads the user's choices with input$name. - When the user changes a menu, Shiny automatically re-runs only the outputs that use it. - reactive() stores a result that several outputs share, so it is calculated once. - eventReactive() waits for a button press. The simulation uses this so it does not restart every time a slider moves.

The six tabs:

Tab What it shows Matches script
Overview The question and a table of first events by group 01
Data Boxplots or bar charts of any variable, compared by a chosen group 02
Survival Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank test, for a chosen grouping 03
Competing risks Cumulative incidence and Gray's test; correct vs naive relapse estimate 04

Simulation Choose the death and censoring rates, run, compare both methods 08

A good live demonstration: on the Simulation tab, set the death rate to 0 and run - both methods agree. Then raise it to 0.30 and run again - the naive method moves well above the red line.

How to read the results

You see Meaning How to write it
HR or SHR = 1 No effect "Sex had no clear effect on relapse."
HR or SHR above 1 Higher risk than the reference group "AML high-risk patients had a higher relapse rate than ALL patients (HR = ...)."
HR or SHR below 1 Lower risk than the reference group "AML low-risk patients had a lower probability of relapse (SHR = ...)."
95% CI includes 1 Not statistically significant "The confidence interval includes 1, so the effect may be zero."
p below 0.05 Statistically significant "Survival differed between disease groups (log-rank p = ...)."
cox.zph p above 0.05 Proportional hazards assumption holds "There was no evidence of non-proportional hazards."

Fill in the real numbers from the files in output/tables/.

Reference groups (what each comparison is against): ALL for disease group, female for sex. Age is per 10 years older.

Forest plot: each dot is an HR or SHR, the horizontal line its 95% confidence interval, and the dashed line at 1 means "no effect". Dots to the right mean higher risk. If a line crosses 1, the effect is not significant. The two colours show the two models for the same variable.

Questions you may be asked

Why not just use Kaplan-Meier for relapse? Because it treats deaths as censored and assumes those patients could still relapse, which overestimates the relapse risk. Our comparison plot and the simulation show this.

What is the difference between censoring and a competing event? A censored patient might still have the event later; a patient with a competing event never can.

Why two models? Cox describes how fast relapse happens among patients still at risk. Fine-Gray describes the overall probability of relapse. They answer different questions and can give different results.

How did you check the Cox model? With the proportional hazards test (cox.zph), based on Schoenfeld residuals.

Why did you not model death as well? The same two models for death would double the output without changing our main conclusions about relapse. It can be done by changing one line (see the comments in scripts 05 and 06).

Why a simulation? Because in real data the true risk is unknown. The simulation shows, with a known answer, that the naive method is biased and the Aalen-Johansen method is not.

Limitations? Small sample (137 patients, and fewer events per group), so confidence intervals are wide. One older dataset. Only a few covariates in the models.
